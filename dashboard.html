Japan — the strategic case and the visa reality New

You're moving to Japan on April 1 regardless. That turns Tokyo from an exotic option into arguably the most logical market on this list — and it comes with one hard constraint that shapes everything.

The visa reality

  • Japan has no working-holiday agreement with the United States. Unlike Australia, there is no self-sponsored route to paid work.
  • Paid internships require a Designated Activities visa, sponsored by the employer. Possible, but few companies will run that process for ten weeks.
  • Unpaid internships of 90 days or less, for students enrolled at an overseas university, can be done on Temporary Visitor status. Jan 4 to Mar 12 is 68 days — comfortably inside that limit.
  • Longer unpaid arrangements use a Cultural Activities visa; not needed at your length.
  • Once you're on a student visa from April, you can work part-time up to 28 hours a week with permission — so a January unpaid stint can convert into paid part-time work in the same company from spring.
This is the case where unpaid is worth it. You said unpaid is acceptable when strategically valuable. A Tokyo stint costs no second relocation, builds a network before your semester starts, gives you a Japanese company on your résumé while you study there, and can convert to paid part-time work in April. Few unpaid roles clear that bar. This one does.

Where to look

  • Japan Dev and TokyoDev — English-first Japanese tech jobs. Engineering-skewed, but the companies listed are exactly the ones that hire foreigners in any function.
  • Coral Capital — Tokyo VC that publishes in English and runs a portfolio job board. The single best entry point into the English-friendly Japanese startup scene.
  • iAgora Tokyo — English-speaker roles and internships.
  • Around 70% of Japanese startups are headquartered in Tokyo, and the internationally-oriented ones actively want native English speakers for overseas market work.

How to position

  • Lead with the April relocation. It removes their biggest objections at once — no visa sponsorship, no housing, no flight risk, and someone who'll still be in the country afterward.
  • Target companies with overseas ambitions. A native English speaker who can write their market research, localize their copy, or handle international partnerships is a scarce and concrete asset.
  • Don't claim Japanese ability you don't have. English-first companies exist and are the right target; overstating language skill fails immediately.
  • Say the words "unpaid is fine" early if it is. It removes the budget conversation entirely and is the fastest path to yes.
The technical-adjacent lane New

You said you want to be close to engineering without being an engineer. That's not a compromise — it's a well-defined career lane with real titles, real ladders, and persistent understaffing. Technical companies need people who can hold a credible conversation with an engineer and a customer, and there are far more of the first than the second. An economics major with a stats minor who can write SQL is unusually well shaped for it.

The actual job titles

  • Solutions Engineer / Sales Engineer (also "pre-sales") — the technical half of a sales team. You demo the product, scope integrations, and answer "can it do X?" No shipping production code. The most direct version of what you described, and the biggest single hiring gap in B2B software.
  • Solutions Architect / Solutions Consultant — the same work post-sale: designing how a customer actually implements the thing.
  • Technical Account Manager — owns a customer relationship where the product is technical. Relationship work with a technical substrate.
  • Deployment Strategist / Forward-Deployed — Palantir's coinage, now widespread. Embedded with a customer, translating their problem into what the product can do. Explicitly non-engineering, explicitly technical.
  • Developer Relations (DevRel) — writing, teaching, community, conference talks, docs. Technical fluency without technical output.
  • Partnerships / Integrations at an API company — you spend your days explaining an API to another company's team. Plaid, Stripe, and Twilio are the archetype.
  • Product Operations and Revenue Operations — the data-and-systems plumbing behind product and sales teams. Heavy SQL, heavy spreadsheets, close to engineering without being in it.

Why it fits you specifically

  • These roles screen for translation ability, not implementation ability. Explaining a regression to a non-technical stakeholder is the same muscle as explaining an API to a procurement team.
  • They're customer-facing and social — the thing you said you wanted — while sitting inside a technical org, so you absorb the domain by proximity.
  • SQL plus product curiosity is genuinely enough to enter. Nobody asks a solutions engineer to pass a coding interview; they ask you to demo something and handle objections.
  • Compensation tracks sales, not support. The ceiling is high and it's an unusually good on-ramp to product management later.

Where these roles concentrate

  • API and infrastructure companies — Plaid, Stripe, Twilio, MongoDB, Modal, Baseten. The product is technical, so the commercial team must be too.
  • Data tooling — Hightouch, Hex, Amplitude, dbt. They sell to data teams, which means your stats background is the qualification rather than a nice-to-have.
  • Dev tools — Vercel, Retool, LangChain. DevRel and technical GTM roles are common and often open to juniors.

How to prepare — it's narrow

  • Learn what an API is, properly. Not how to build one — how to read documentation, make a request in Postman, and explain what a webhook does. A weekend gets you there and it's most of the technical bar.
  • Practise the demo. Pick a product you like, record yourself giving a five-minute walkthrough to an imagined customer. Painful, and the single highest-return rehearsal for this lane.
  • Learn to handle "can it do X?" when the answer is no. The good answer names the workaround and the tradeoff. Interviewers test exactly this.
  • SQL still applies — it's the shared language of every one of these roles.
The pitch in one line: "I'm not an engineer, but I can read the docs, run the demo, and tell your engineers what the customer actually meant." Say that in an email to a 20-person API company and you will get a reply.
Strategy & economic consulting New

Two different jobs share the word "strategy", and they recruit on completely different logic. Both are open to you; one of them is arguably the best pure fit on this whole board.

Economic consulting — the closest match to your degree

  • Analysis Group, Cornerstone Research, Charles River Associates, Compass Lexecon. They do litigation, antitrust and policy work built directly on econometrics.
  • Here your coursework is the job rather than a proxy for it. Regression, causal inference, and the ability to explain a model to a non-technical audience are the daily work, not a screening hurdle.
  • They actively recruit economics and statistics undergraduates and train them. You are the target profile, not an unusual applicant — which is rarely true anywhere else on this board.
  • Less brand-competitive than the strategy houses while using the same skills, and it opens both consulting and graduate-school doors later.
  • Ask specifically about off-cycle. These firms staff by case, not by cohort, so a ten-week block in Q1 is far easier for them to absorb than for a company running a fixed programme.

Strategy & Operations — the startup version

  • Uber, DoorDash, Flexport and most marketplace companies. Uber invented the modern shape of this role.
  • The job: own a metric, find why it's moving, ship the fix. Heavy SQL, heavy commercial judgement, very little ceremony.
  • It sits exactly between the data and business tracks you're targeting, which makes it a natural landing spot for an econ-plus-stats profile that doesn't want to be purely one or the other.
  • Deployment Strategist (Palantir's coinage) is the technical-adjacent variant — embedded with a customer, translating their problem into the product.

Interview form — this is where strategy differs

  • Case interviews. Both lanes use them, but differently. Consulting cases are structured and framework-friendly. S&O cases are usually a real metric problem from the business: "orders in Chicago dropped 8% last month, what happened?"
  • Structure out loud, state assumptions, and do the arithmetic without a calculator. Practise mental maths — candidates lose more cases to shaky arithmetic than to weak frameworks.
  • Economic consulting adds a technical screen: interpreting regression output, and explaining a statistical result to a lawyer or a judge. Rehearse the plain-English version.
  • Expect a SQL screen for any S&O role.
If you only chase one new thing from this list, make it economic consulting. It is the only category here where an economics major with a statistics minor is the preferred candidate rather than an adjacent one — and the case-by-case staffing model means your fixed January-to-March window is a mild scheduling question rather than a dealbreaker.

SQL — the gate Priority 1

Required for every data and growth role here, and quietly expected in BD and PM roles at data-literate companies. The one item where being self-taught is completely normal.

Learn

  • Core: SELECT, WHERE, GROUP BY, HAVING, all four JOIN types, subqueries, CTEs.
  • Window functions — ROW_NUMBER, RANK, LAG/LEAD, running totals, PARTITION BY.
  • Applied patterns: cohort retention, funnel conversion by step, month-over-month growth, deduplication, nth-highest value.
  • Enough schema literacy to ask "what's the grain of this table?" — that question alone signals experience.

Practice

  • Mode's SQL Tutorial — free, browser-based, business-analytics framing rather than CS framing. Best start for you specifically.
  • DataLemur — real interview questions, substantial free tier.
  • StrataScratch — company-tagged problems.
  • Thirty minutes daily for four weeks beats four long weekends.

Interview form

  • Live-coded, 20–30 minutes, two to three escalating questions.
  • Narrate as you write. They're evaluating decomposition more than syntax.
  • Ask about nulls, duplicates, and ties before writing. Volunteering that is a senior signal.
Python, spreadsheets, and BI tooling Priority 2

Working fluency, not software engineering. The question is whether you can load a messy CSV and answer something with it.

Learn

  • pandas — read, merge, groupby, pivot, handle missing data. Eighty percent of the job.
  • Basic plotting and Jupyter notebooks as a working medium.
  • Spreadsheets, seriously. Pivot tables, XLOOKUP, clean models. At startups and in BD, more real analysis happens here than anywhere else.
  • One BI tool conversationally: Tableau or Looker.

Skip

  • Machine learning and MLOps. Not what these roles hire for, and claiming it invites questions you can't win.
Statistics & experimentation — your edge Your strength

Where the stats minor earns its keep. Most growth candidates can't explain a confidence interval; most data candidates can't explain one to a non-technical stakeholder. Doing both is the entire pitch.

Sharpen

  • A/B testing end to end: hypothesis, randomization, sample size and power, minimum detectable effect, when to stop, what breaks when you peek early.
  • Interpreting regression for a business audience — coefficients, what R² does and doesn't say, causation without sounding like a textbook.
  • Selection bias, survivorship bias, Simpson's paradox. Econometrics gives you these; the skill is naming them in a product context.
  • Multiple comparisons — why testing twenty metrics guarantees a false positive.

Interview form

  • "Explain a p-value to a PM who doesn't know statistics." Asked constantly. Rehearse until it's 30 seconds and jargon-free.
  • "The result was significant, should we ship?" The right answer explores effect size, cost, duration, novelty effects.
  • "This metric dropped 15% overnight." Structure it: real or instrumentation, all users or a segment, what changed that day.
Growth Ramp · Attentive · Brex · Linktree

Quantitative marketing. Closest function to your natural combination, and where the econ background is a differentiator rather than something to explain.

Know

  • The funnel: acquisition, activation, retention, referral, revenue. Know which stage a company is actually constrained by — that's the question behind most growth interviews.
  • Retention curves. A curve that flattens means product-market fit; one that decays to zero means a leaky bucket, not a growth problem.
  • LTV, CAC, payback period, and why CAC-to-LTV misleads when growth is fast.
  • Cohort analysis, north star metrics, vanity versus actionable metrics.
  • Channels: paid, organic, referral, sales-led, and product-led growth as a motion.

Read

  • Lenny's Newsletter — the shared vocabulary of growth and product. If you read one thing, read this.
  • Andrew Chen — network effects, growth loops, cold-start problems.
  • Reforge's public essays are free and the best growth material anywhere.

Interview form

  • "How would you grow our product?" Don't list tactics. Identify the constraint, propose experiments against it, name the metric and how you'd measure.
  • Estimation questions — show assumptions explicitly; auditable reasoning beats the number.
  • Expect a SQL screen attached. Growth roles are data roles wearing a marketing hat.
Business development & sales Alloy · Justworks · AlertMedia

Interview formats — what each actually looks like

Behavioral

  • STAR: situation, task, action, result. Six to eight stories covering conflict, failure, leadership, ambiguity, persuasion, quantified impact.
  • Always quantify. "Grew the club from 15 to 60" lands; "helped grow the club" doesn't.
  • Have a real failure with a real lesson. "I'm a perfectionist" is a wasted answer.

Technical screens

  • SQL live-coding, 20–30 minutes, narrate throughout.
  • Take-home analysis: graded on framing and communication far more than technique. Lead with the answer, then the method.

Case & product

  • Structure out loud before diving in. State assumptions. Check in before going deeper. Silence is the enemy.

Your questions for them

  • "What would you want done by March 12?" — reframes you as someone who ships, and surfaces the end-date conversation on your terms.
  • Ask what success looks like at 30 days. Never ask what their homepage answers.
What to build — one portfolio project

One real project beats three more courses. It gives every interview a concrete thing to discuss and proves what your transcript only implies.

What makes it work

  • A real question with a real answer, not a tutorial rerun. "I analyzed Austin housing permit data to test whether new supply moved rents" beats a Titanic notebook by a mile.
  • Messy public data, so you demonstrate cleaning — the actual job.
  • SQL or pandas, one statistical claim you defend, and a recommendation rather than a chart dump.
  • A README a non-technical reader can follow. The writing is half the signal.

Angles that fit you

  • Anything economic — labor markets, housing, pricing, elasticity. Plays your major as a strength.
  • Something about a target company: model their pricing, size their market, analyze public reviews. Doubles as outreach material.
  • Japan angle: analyze a market you're about to live in. Instant credibility with every Tokyo company you email.
  • Datasets: BLS, Census, FRED, city open-data portals, e-Stat for Japan.
Scope it to two weekends. An unfinished ambitious project is worth nothing; a finished narrow one is worth a lot.

How to research a company before applying

Thirty focused minutes. The difference between a reply and silence.

The pass

  • Use the product. Sign up for the free tier. Non-negotiable, and almost nobody does it.
  • Read their other job postings. What they hire for reveals what they're struggling with — three sales roles and no engineers means distribution is the constraint.
  • Pricing page — the customer, the deal size, self-serve versus sales-led.
  • Crunchbase — last round, size, date. Recently funded means hiring; 30 months ago may not.
  • Founder podcast appearances. They repeat their strategy verbatim; twenty minutes gives you their exact framing.

Turn it into an email

  • Open with the observation, not yourself. "Your onboarding asks for company size before explaining the product — I'd guess that costs activation" earns a reply.
  • State the dates plainly: January 4 to March 12, in person.
  • One concrete offer. Five sentences total.

Preparation

What to learn, build, read, and rehearse between now and January — organized by the function you're applying into. The honest summary: you have roughly five months, which is more than enough, and the binding constraint is SQL, not anything about your major. An economics degree with a stats minor is a strong hand for these roles. It just has to be legible to a recruiter screening on keywords.

Start here — the five-month plan Read first

Sequenced so each thing you learn is usable in the applications you're sending that same month. Don't study in parallel with applying — study ahead of applying.

August — close the SQL gap

  • SQL is the single highest-return thing you can do, and it isn't close. It's the keyword that gets an econ major past an analytics résumé screen, and it's the live-coded portion of nearly every data interview. Three to four weeks of daily practice is genuinely enough to interview well.
  • Get to the point where JOIN, GROUP BY, CTEs, and window functions are automatic. Window functions are the actual dividing line — most candidates stall there, so fluency reads as real experience.
  • In parallel, draft your outreach templates. September is for sending, not writing.

September — build the portfolio project, start outreach

  • One end-to-end analysis you can talk about for ten minutes. It does more work in an interview than any coursework line on your résumé.

SQL — the gate Priority 1

Required for every data and growth role on your list, and quietly expected in BD and PM roles at data-literate companies. This is the one item where being self-taught is completely normal and nobody will ask where you learned it.

Learn

  • Core: SELECT, WHERE, GROUP BY, HAVING, all four JOIN types, subqueries, CTEs (WITH).
  • Window functions — ROW_NUMBER, RANK, LAG/LEAD, running totals, PARTITION BY. Where most candidates stop and where interviews concentrate.
  • Applied patterns: cohort retention, funnel conversion by step, month-over-month growth, deduplication, finding the nth-highest value.
  • Enough schema literacy to ask "what's the grain of this table?" — that question alone signals experience.

Practice

  • Mode's SQL Tutorial — free, browser-based, business-analytics framing rather than CS framing. Best starting point for you specifically.
  • DataLemur — real interview questions from real companies, free tier is substantial.
  • StrataScratch — company-tagged problems, good for targeted prep.
  • Daily beats weekly. Thirty minutes a day for four weeks outperforms four long weekends.

Interview form

  • Live-coded in a shared editor, 20–30 minutes, usually two to three questions escalating in difficulty.
  • Narrate as you write. They're evaluating how you decompose a problem more than whether the syntax runs. Saying "I'll get the per-user first purchase, then join back" is worth more than silently producing correct output.
  • Ask about edge cases before writing: nulls, duplicates, ties. Volunteering that is a senior signal.
Python, spreadsheets, and BI tooling Priority 2

You need working fluency, not software engineering. Nobody is asking you to pass a LeetCode gauntlet for an analytics internship — they're asking whether you can load a messy CSV and answer a question with it.

Learn

  • pandas — read, merge, groupby, pivot, handle missing data. This is 80% of the job.
  • Basic plotting (matplotlib or seaborn) and Jupyter notebooks as a working medium.
  • Spreadsheets, seriously. Pivot tables, INDEX/MATCH or XLOOKUP, and building a clean model. Underrated — at startups and in BD roles, more real analysis happens in a spreadsheet than anywhere else.
  • One BI tool at a conversational level: Tableau or Looker. Enough to say what a dashboard should show and why.

Skip for now

  • Machine learning, deep learning, and MLOps. Not what these roles hire for, and claiming them invites questions you can't win.
  • Software engineering fundamentals beyond writing readable scripts.
Statistics & experimentation — your edge Your strength

This is where the stats minor earns its keep. Most growth and BD candidates cannot explain a confidence interval. Most data candidates can't explain one to a non-technical stakeholder. Being able to do both is the entire pitch.

Sharpen

  • A/B testing end to end: hypothesis, randomization, sample size and power, minimum detectable effect, when to stop, what breaks when you peek at results early.
  • Interpreting regression output for a business audience — coefficient meaning, what R² does and doesn't tell you, why correlation isn't causation in a way that doesn't sound like a textbook.
  • Selection bias, survivorship bias, Simpson's paradox. Econometrics gives you these already; the skill is naming them in a product context.
  • The multiple-comparisons problem — why testing twenty metrics guarantees a false positive.

Interview form

  • "Explain a p-value to a product manager who doesn't know statistics." Asked constantly. Rehearse it out loud until it's 30 seconds and jargon-free.
  • "We ran a test, the result was significant, should we ship?" The correct answer explores effect size, cost, duration, and novelty effects — not just the p-value.
  • "This metric dropped 15% overnight. What do you do?" A diagnostic case. Structure it: is it real or instrumentation, is it all users or a segment, what changed on that date.
Growth Ramp · Attentive · Brex · Linktree

Quantitative marketing. The closest function to your natural combination, and the one where the econ background is a differentiator rather than something to explain away.

Know

  • The funnel: acquisition, activation, retention, referral, revenue. Know which stage a given company is actually constrained by — that's the question behind most growth interviews.
  • Retention curves. Understand why a curve that flattens means product-market fit and one that decays to zero means you have a leaky bucket, not a growth problem.
  • LTV, CAC, payback period, and why CAC-to-LTV ratios mislead when growth is fast.
  • Cohort analysis, north star metrics, and the difference between a vanity metric and an actionable one.
  • Channel basics: paid vs organic vs referral vs sales-led, and product-led growth as a motion.

Read

  • Lenny's Newsletter — the default shared vocabulary of growth and product people. Free tier is plenty. If you read one thing, read this.
  • Andrew Chen's essays — network effects, growth loops, cold-start problems.
  • Reforge writes the best growth material anywhere; the courses are expensive, but their public essays are free and worth reading.

Interview form

  • "How would you grow [our product]?" — Don't list tactics. Identify the constraint first, then propose experiments against it, with the metric you'd move and how you'd measure it.
  • Estimation and sizing questions. Show your assumptions explicitly; the number matters less than whether your reasoning is auditable.
  • Expect a SQL screen attached. Growth roles are data roles wearing a marketing hat.
Business development & sales Alloy · Justworks · AlertMedia · SafetyCulture

The most learnable track on this list and the one where hustle substitutes most directly for credentials. It's also the one where a junior can produce measurable value inside a three-month window, which makes it an easier ask for a January start.

Know

  • The pipeline: prospecting, qualification, discovery, demo, proposal, negotiation, close. Know what happens at each stage and what causes deals to stall.
  • Qualification frameworks — MEDDIC and BANT. Know them well enough to name them; interviewers use them as shorthand.
  • The difference between inbound and outbound, and between an SDR (books meetings) and an AE (closes). Startups blur these; know which you're being offered.
  • CRM mechanics — Salesforce or HubSpot. Even surface familiarity helps.
  • Founder-led sales at early-stage companies: no playbook exists yet, and you'd be helping write it. That's the pitch to a seed-stage startup.

Read

  • Founding Sales by Pete Kazanjy — free online, written specifically for startup sales from zero. The single most relevant book for the roles you're targeting.
  • SPIN Selling for discovery question technique; The Challenger Sale for the framing most B2B teams reference.

Interview form

  • Mock cold call or role-play. Extremely common and it is where most candidates fall apart. Practice out loud with a friend. Being bad at this is fine; being visibly unwilling to try is disqualifying.
  • "Sell me this pen" variants — the real test is whether you ask questions before pitching. Most people pitch. Don't.
  • They're screening for coachability and resilience above polish. Say what you'd do differently after a rejection.
Do this before any BD interview: actually cold-email ten people this month about anything. Being able to say "I sent 40 cold emails and 6 replied, here's what I changed after the first 20" beats every framework you could memorize.
Strategic partnerships Vanta · Immutable · Sierra

Hardest track to study for and the one with the fewest junior openings — usually staffed by people who did BD first. Worth targeting where a company is explicitly partnership-led.

Know

  • Partnership types: channel/reseller (they sell for you), technology/integration (products connect), co-marketing, and strategic alliances. Confusing these is the tell of someone who hasn't done it.
  • Why companies partner instead of building or buying — distribution access, credibility, filling a product gap.
  • How partnerships get measured: sourced pipeline, influenced revenue, integration adoption. Notoriously hard to attribute, and saying so shows you understand the function.
  • Basic contract vocabulary: MSA, SLA, revenue share, exclusivity, term and termination.

Preparation

  • Pick a target company and map its actual partner ecosystem from its own site — integrations page, partner directory, press releases. Walking in with that map is a stronger opener than any framework.
  • Then form a view: which partner is underleveraged, and what would you do about it? Have one specific answer ready.
Product management Notion · Typeform

The most competitive track per seat, and the one where being pre-senior hurts most — most APM programs recruit graduating seniors. Realistic as a startup role, hard as a branded program.

Know

  • Product sense — the ability to reason from a user problem to a solution and defend the tradeoff. Not memorizable; developed by critiquing products deliberately.
  • Prioritization frameworks (RICE, impact/effort) as vocabulary, not as a crutch. Reciting a framework instead of thinking is the classic failure mode.
  • Writing specs, defining success metrics before launch, and knowing what a PM actually does versus what they're imagined to do.
  • Enough technical literacy to talk credibly with engineers: what an API is, what a database does, why something is hard.

Read

  • Decode and Conquer (Lewis Lin) and Cracking the PM Interview (McDowell & Bavaro) — the two standards.
  • Lenny's Newsletter again; it's as much a PM resource as a growth one.
  • Stratechery for strategic reasoning about why products and business models work.

Interview form

  • Product design: "Design X for Y user." Structure it — clarify the goal, pick a user segment, list their pain points, propose solutions, choose one and say why, define the success metric.
  • Product critique: "What's your favorite product and how would you improve it?" Pick something unglamorous you genuinely use. Everyone picks Spotify.
  • Analytical: "Metric X dropped, diagnose it." Overlaps heavily with the data track — your advantage.
The startup generalist track Highest leverage

Founder's associate, chief of staff, first business hire. Given your November YC sweep, this is the most probable path to an actual January start — so prepare for it hardest, even though it's the least formalized.

Know

  • How early-stage companies work: what pre-seed, seed, Series A actually mean, what runway is, what a founder is worried about at each stage.
  • Why a two-person company hires a generalist — they're drowning, not building a career ladder. Your pitch is relief, not potential.
  • Enough of everything: pull a number, write a cold email, take a customer call, put together a deck, keep a spreadsheet honest.

Read

  • The YC Library and Startup School — free, and it is exactly the worldview your interviewer holds.
  • Paul Graham's essays — Do Things That Don't Scale and Default Alive or Default Dead in particular.

Interview form

  • A conversation, not a structured loop. Often the founder, often 30 minutes, often a decision the same week.
  • They screen for: can you start immediately, will you do unglamorous work, do you understand what they're building, will you need managing.
  • Come with a specific observation about their product. Sign up, use it, find something broken or missing, mention it. That single move separates you from everyone sending a résumé.
Your Q1 availability is the pitch. Their summer interns went back to school in September, they're understaffed through Q1, and almost nobody is available in January. Lead the email with the dates, not with your GPA.
Interview formats — what each actually looks like

Behavioral

  • STAR: situation, task, action, result. Write six to eight stories covering conflict, failure, leadership, ambiguity, persuasion, and quantified impact. They recombine to cover almost any question.
  • Always quantify the result. "Grew the club from 15 to 60 members" lands; "helped grow the club" doesn't.
  • Have a real failure story with a real lesson. "I'm a perfectionist" is a wasted answer.

Technical screens

  • SQL live-coding: 20–30 minutes, two to three escalating questions, narrate throughout.
  • Take-home analysis: a messy dataset and an open question. Graded on framing and communication far more than technique. Lead with the answer, then the method.

Case & product

  • Structure out loud before diving in. State assumptions. Check in: "does that framing make sense before I go deeper?"
  • Silence is the enemy — thinking out loud is the deliverable.

Your questions for them

  • Ask what success looks like at 30 and 90 days. Signals you're thinking about delivery, not just getting hired.
  • For a three-month stint specifically: "What would you want done by March?" Reframes you as someone who ships.
  • Never ask something answerable from their homepage.
What to build — one portfolio project

One real project does more than three more courses. It gives every interview a concrete thing to discuss and proves the skills your transcript only implies.

What makes it work

  • A real question with a real answer, not a tutorial rerun. "I analyzed Austin housing permit data to test whether new supply moved rents" beats a Titanic notebook by a mile.
  • Messy public data, so you demonstrate cleaning — the actual job.
  • Uses SQL or pandas, contains at least one statistical claim you defend, and ends with a recommendation rather than a chart dump.
  • Written up in a README a non-technical reader can follow. The writing is half the signal.

Angles that fit you specifically

  • Anything economic — labor markets, housing, pricing, elasticity. Plays your major as a strength rather than a detour.
  • Something about a company you're targeting: model their pricing page, size their market, analyze their public reviews. Doubles as outreach material.
  • Public datasets: BLS, Census, FRED, city open-data portals, Kaggle for structured sets.
Scope it to two weekends. An unfinished ambitious project is worth nothing; a finished narrow one is worth a lot. Ship it, then write it up.
News & staying current

Not for its own sake — so you can hold a conversation with a founder about their market without sounding like you skimmed their homepage that morning. Twenty minutes a day.

Core

  • Stratechery (Ben Thompson) — the best strategic analysis in tech. Free Monday articles are enough.
  • Lenny's Newsletter — product and growth, and the vocabulary your interviewers use.
  • The Information — best original reporting in tech. Paywalled, but they run steep student pricing; worth asking.
  • Axios Pro Rata — daily funding and M&A in five minutes. Free.
  • Not Boring (Packy McCormick) — long-form company deep dives, genuinely fun to read.

By market

  • AI: Import AI (Jack Clark) and The Batch (Andrew Ng) — both free, both keep you literate without pretending to be a researcher.
  • Fintech: Fintech Brainfood (Simon Taylor). Directly relevant to Ramp, Mercury, Brex, Alloy, Airwallex.

  • Australia: Australian Financial Review tech section, Startup Daily, SmartCompany. Read these before any ANZ interview — the market has its own reference points.
  • Spain: Novobrief and El Referente cover the Barcelona and Madrid startup scenes.
Follow founders, not publications. For any company you're targeting, find the founder on X or LinkedIn and read their last six months. It's the highest-density preparation available and it's free.
How to research a company before applying

Thirty focused minutes per company. Do this before writing any outreach email — it's the difference between a reply and silence.

The pass

  • Use the product. Sign up for the free tier. Non-negotiable, and almost nobody does it.
  • Read their other job postings. What they're hiring for reveals what they're struggling with — three sales roles and no engineers means distribution is the constraint.
  • Pricing page — tells you the customer, the deal size, and whether it's self-serve or sales-led.
  • Engineering and company blog — what they're proud of.
  • Crunchbase — last round, size, date, investors. A company that raised recently is hiring; one that raised 30 months ago may not be.
  • Founder podcast appearances. Founders repeat their strategy verbatim across interviews; twenty minutes gives you their exact framing.

Turn it into an email

  • Open with the specific observation, not with yourself. "Your onboarding asks for a company size before explaining the product — I'd guess that's costing activation" earns a reply.
  • State the dates plainly: available January through March, flexible on the end.
  • One concrete offer of what you'd do. Short. Five sentences total, not fifteen.
